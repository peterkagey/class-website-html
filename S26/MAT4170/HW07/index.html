Exercise 3 [6] This is a long exercise, but it is only worth six points because you can do this problem by following along with the asynchronous lecture which will be available before Monday at 3PM.

Let $G$ be a group. Then we say that $a \in G$ is conjugate to $b \in G$ if there exists $g \in G$ such that $a = g b g^{-1}$. The relation is conjugate to is an equivalence relation, so it allows us to partition $G$ into equivalence classes (which we call conjugacy classes).

Intuitively, $a$ and $b$ are conjugate if performing $a$ from one perspective is the same as performing $b$ from another perspective. As an example, a horizontal flip and a vertical flip of a square are conjugate in $D_4$ because if you're standing $90^\circ$ to the right of me and I do a horizontal flip of the square, it will look like a vertical flip from your perspective—but a horizontal flip to me will never look like a $180^\circ$ rotation to you.

In the following exercises you will be looking at conjugacy classes of the symmetry group of the hexagon.

  1. Prove that $e$ is in its own conjugacy class.
  2. Find an example of $g \in D_6$ such that $r^2 = g r^4 g^{-1}$.
  3. Find an example of $g \in D_6$ such that $f = g r^2f g^{-1}$.
  4. Prove that if $\phi\colon D_6 \to H$ is a homomorphism, and $g_1$ is conjugate to $g_2$ in $D_6$, then $\phi(g_1)$ is conjugate to $\phi(g_2)$ in $H$.
  5. Prove that if $\phi\colon D_6 \to H$ is a homomorphism, $H$ is an abelian group, and $g_1$ is conjugate to $g_2$ in $D_6$, then $\phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2)$.
  6. Let $\Phi\colon D_6 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be defined by $\Phi(r) = 0$ and $\Phi(f) = 1$, and use this to show that $r^nf$ is not conjugate to $r^m$ for any integers $n$ and $m$.
  7. Let $\psi\colon D_6 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be defined by $\psi(r) = 1$ and $\psi(f) = 0$, and use this to prove that none of the elements in $\{r, rf, r^3, r^3f, r^5, r^5f\}$ are conjugate to any of the elements in $\{e, f, r^2, r^2f, r^4, r^4f\}$.
  8. Let $\varphi\colon D_6 \to S_3$ be the homomorphism defined by $\varphi(f) = (1\ 2)$ and $\varphi(r) = (1\ 2\ 3)$. Note that $S_3$ is not an abelian group, but use this homomorphism to show that $r^3 \in D_6$ is in its own equivalence class.
  9. Partition the dihedral group of the hexagon \[ D_6 = \langle r, f \mid r^6 = f^2 = (rf)^2 = e \rangle \] into conjugacy classes. You don't have to prove every piece of this, but you do have to make a note of any claims that you made that you didn't prove.

Choice Exercise 5 [≤13] (true/false)
  1. [3] Attempt the following true/false questions and give brief explanations for your reasoning.
    1. If $\varphi \colon D_8 \to D_4$ is a homomorphism, then \[ D_8/\ker(\varphi) \cong D_4. \]
    2. If $G$ is an infinite group with $H \leq G$, then the index of $H$ in $G$ is finite: $[G:H] < \infty$.
    3. Let $G \times H$ be a direct product of groups, and let \[ N = \{(g, e_H) \mid g \in G\} \cong G. \] Then $N \triangleleft G \times H$ and the quotient group $(G \times H)/N$ is isomorphic to $H$.
    4. If $G$ is a group, and $N$ is a normal subgroup, then there exists a subgroup $H$ in $G$ such that $G/N \cong H$.
    5. If $\varphi\colon G \to H$ is an homomorphism with kernel $K = \ker(\varphi)$, then the map $\psi\colon G/K \to \operatorname{Im}(\varphi)$ that sends $\psi(aK) = \varphi(a)$ is also a homomorphism.
    6. If $g \in G$ has order $k$ and $h \in H$ has order $k$, then there exists an isomorphism $\varphi\colon \langle g \rangle \to \langle h \rangle$.
    7. If the only normal subgroups of $G$ are $\{e\} \triangleleft G$ and $G \triangleleft G$, then $G$ is the trivial group (i.e. $|G| = 1$.)
    8. If $G$ is an abelian group and $H \leq G$, then $H \triangleleft G$ and $G/H$ is abelian.
    9. If $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic, then any homomorphism from $G$ to $H$ is an isomorphism.
    10. If $G$ is a finite group with $|G| \geq 3$ and $H$ is an infinite group, then any homomorphism $\varphi\colon G \to H$ fails to be one-to-one.
  2. [≤10] (One point per corrected answer.) Expand the section below to see the answers. If you got the answer or explanation wrong, write the correct answer here and say something about why you got it wrong originally.
    True/false answers
    1. False. Consider the trivial homomorphism.
    2. False. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}$ and $H = 2\mathbb{Z}$.
    3. True! Let $\phi\colon G \times H \to H$ be defined by $\phi(g,h) = h$. Then $\ker(\phi) = N$, and $\operatorname{Im}(\phi) = H$.
    4. False. The idea here is that statements in group theory tend to be false unless there's a good reason for them to be true. The smallest counterexample arises from the warmup we did on Wednesday of week 8, where we constructed a quotient group of the quaternion group $Q_8$ by the subgroup $\langle -1 \rangle$ that was isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. However, $Q_8$ only has one element of order $2$ (namely $-1 \in Q_8$), so it doesn't have a subgroup that is isomorphic to the image $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.
    5. True! It's the isomorphism we used in the first isomorphism theorem. (And all isomorphisms are homomorphisms!)
    6. True! The map $\varphi(g) = h$ extends to an isomorphism.
    7. False. The easiest counterexample is $C_2$.
    8. True! The subgroup $H$ is normal because $gh = hg$ for all $g \in G$ and $h \in H$, and $aH * bH = (a * b)H = (b * a)H = bH * aH$.
    9. False. The trivial homomorphism is not an isomorphism.
    10. False. Let $G = \{1, i, -1, -i\} \leq \mathbb{C}$ and $H = \mathbb{C}$. Then the ``inclusion map'' $\varphi\colon G \to H$ where $\varphi(g) = g$ is one-to-one.

Choice Exercise 8 [≤13] ⭐️ (Generating \(S_n\)) Last time one student chose this as their favorite choice exercise.

In the symmetric group \(S_n\), an adjacent transposition is a \(2\)-cycle where the indices differ by \(1\), which we write as \(s_i = (i\ i+1)\). For instance, \(s_2 = (2\ 3)\) or \(s_8 = (8\ 9)\) are adjacent transpositions, but \((1\ 9)\) is not. It turns out that every permutation in \(S_n\) can be written as a product of transpositions.

  1. [1] Write the word \(524316\) as a product of adjacent transpositions. (Hint: what is the first letter of \(s_1 \circ s_2 \circ s_3 \circ s_4\) written as a word?)
  2. [1] Check this result in WolframAlpha by inputting permutation (1 2)(2 3) but with the transpositions found above. (Note that WolframAlpha reads the permutations left-to-right!)
  3. [3] If a permutation is written as a sequence of adjacent transpositions and there is no way to write it as a shorter sequence adjacent transpositions, then the sequence of adjacent transpositions is called a reduced word. (Note: a reduced word is a product of transpositions, not to be confused with writing a permutation in word notation.) We can move between reduced words by using the two identities:
    1. \(s_i \circ s_j = s_j \circ s_i\) if \(|i - j| \geq 2\)
    2. \(s_i \circ s_{i+1} \circ s_i = s_{i+1} \circ s_i \circ s_{i+1}\) for all \(1 \leq i < n - 1\).
    Prove these two identities, and use them to give an example of a reduced word that is different from the reduced word \( s_1 \circ s_3 \circ s_2 \circ s_3 \circ s_4 \circ s_3 \circ s_2 \circ s_1 \) but which is equal as a permutation.
  4. [5] Prove that every permutation in \(S_n\) can be written as a product of adjacent transpositions, and thus \[ \langle s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{n-1} \rangle = S_n. \]
  5. [3] While \(S_6\) cannot be generated by a single element (because it is not a cyclic group), it can be generated by just two elements: \[ \langle (1\ 2), \underbrace{(1\ 2\ 3\ 4\ 5\ 6)}_c \rangle = S_6. \] Write the word \(524316\) in terms of \(s_1\) and \(c\).
