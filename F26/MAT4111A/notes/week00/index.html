Welcome to Putnam Prep!

MAT 4111A: Putnam Prep is a course designed to help you learn the kinds of contest math that appears on the William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition. The Putnam is a six-hour undergraduate competition that can only be taken four times. It consists of two three-hour parts, each with six very difficult questions. (In 2024, 3988 students took the exam, and 2807 of them scored 0/120, 1171 of them scored 10/120 or better, and only 100 students scored 48/120 or better.)

Contest math requires a different (but overlapping) set of skills compared to what you learn in your math classes at CPP. Here, we will learn some of the techniques and ideas that historically have appeared in math contests. I encourage students to take the Putnam because I think there is great value in thinking deeply about rich, difficult problems.

When I took the 2013 Putnam, I got home and couldn't stop thinking about Problem B2. The reason I went to graduate school is in large part due to this problem, and ultimately a 20 year old conjecture related to this problem appeared in my student Kai Rajesh's senior thesis, which I supervised.

Today's plan

In future weeks, we will work to develop some of the techniques that are useful in contest math. Today, we will try to get a sense of what math contests are like by working on some example problems for an hour. Afterward, we will discuss our ideas with each other.

Four example problems

Problem 1.

This problem comes from the AMC 12, which is a math competition for high school students.

In $\Delta ABC$, $\angle ABC = 90^\circ$ and $BA = BC = \sqrt{2}$. Points $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_{2024}$ lie on hypotenuse $\overline{AC}$ so that $$AP_1= P_1P_2 = P_2P_3 = \dots = P_{2023}P_{2024} = P_{2024}C.$$ What is the length of the vector sum\[\overrightarrow{BP_1} + \overrightarrow{BP_2} + \overrightarrow{BP_3} + \dots + \overrightarrow{BP_{2024}}?\]

2024 AMC 12A, Problem 7

Problem 2.

This problem is from the (now defunct) Virginia Tech Regional Mathematics Contest, which is an undergraduate exam, and the first contest I ever participated in. This was my favorite question from the exam.

Power tower

Define $f(n)$ for a positive integer by $f(1) = 3$ and $f(n+1) = 3^{f(n)}$. What are the last two digits of $f(2012)$?

2012 Virginia Tech Regional Mathematics Contest, question 4

Problem 3.

This is from the Putnam the year I took it. Problem A1 is the first problem in the morning session, and it's typically designed to be the most approachable. (I didn't get this one!)

Recall that a regular icosahedron is a convex polyhedron having 12 vertices and 20 faces; the faces are congruent equilateral triangles. On each face of a regular icosahedron is written a nonnegative integer such that the sum of all 20 integers is 39.
Show that there are two faces that share a vertex and have the same integer written on them.

2013 Putnam Exam, Problem A1

Problem 4.

This is the sixth problem of the morning session of the 2024 Putnam. Problems broadly get more difficult as you go within each section, so while this one appears to just be about Taylor series expansion and determinants, it's really tough! None of the 3988 students got 10/10, and only one student got more than 1/10!

Let $c_0,c_1,c_2,\dots$ be the sequence defined so that \[ \frac{1-3x-\sqrt{1-14x+9x^2}}{4} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_k x^k \] for sufficiently small $x$. For a positive integer $n$, let $A$ be the $n$-by-$n$ matrix with $i,j$-entry $c_{i+j-1}$ for $i$ and $j$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Find the determinant of $A$.

2024 Putnam Exam, Problem A6
