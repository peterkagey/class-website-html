Exercise 5 [4] (Applying Lagrange's theorem)
  1. As formally or as informally as you'd like, prove that if \(g\) is an element of a group with a finite order \(|g| = k\), then the order of the subgroup generated by \(g\) is also \(k\), namely \(|\langle g \rangle| = k\).
  2. A cube has \(24\) rotational symmetries (i.e. non mirror-image symmetries), and we call this symmetry group \(O\) for "(rotational) octahedral symmetry." What are the possible orders of subgroups of \(O\)? (e.g., can there exist an order-\(7\) subgroup of \(O\)?)
  3. Suppose that I have three hexagons on the table: one red, one green, and one blue. Every time I leave the classroom, and a student comes up and applies the same symmetry \((g_1, g_2, g_3) \in D_6 \times D_6 \times D_6\) to them. Prove that the 12th time I enter the classroom, the hexagons are precisely in their starting position.
  4. Bézout's identity tells us that the elements of \((\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times\) are precisely the positive integers \(1 \leq k < n\) that are coprime to \(n\), meaning that \(\gcd(n,k)=1\).
    Euler's phi function \(\phi(n)\) counts the positive integers less than \(n\) that are coprime to \(n\).
    Euler's Theorem tells us that if \(a\) is coprime to \(n\) then \[ a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n. \]
    1. Use your work from last week to show that \(\phi(24) = 8\), and use Lagrange's theorem to compute \(13^9 = 10604499373 \pmod{24}.\)
    2. Use the facts from the previous parts (together with theorems you know and love from class) to prove Euler's Theorem! (This was my favorite proof in undergrad! 😊)

Choice Exercise 7 [2] (Quiz review) Look over last week's quizzes and solutions, and write a sentence or two about a mistake or misconception you had on the quiz for one point per quiz.
  1. The quiz from Tuesday of week 4 asked you about cosets of \(D_4\).
    Answer key.
    1. \[ \begin{align*} H &= \{e, rf, r^2, r^3f\} \\ rH &= \{r, r^2f, r^3, f\} \end{align*} \]
    2. These "other" cosets are just different names for \(rH\). \[ \begin{align*} r^3fH &= \{r^3f, r^2, rf, e\} \\ r^2H &= \{r^2, r^3f, e, rf\} \end{align*} \]
  2. The quiz from Thursday of week 4 asked you to give an example of a coset that is not a subgroup and to prove that a coset \(aH\) is a subgroup if and only if \(a \in H\).
    Answer key.
    1. In a certain sense, most cosets are not subgroups! Pick your favorite group \(G\), your favorite (improper) subgroup \(H\), and any element that is in \(G\) but not \(H\).
      I'll pick \(G = C_4\) and \(H = \langle r^2 \rangle = \{e, r^2\}\). Note that \(rH = \{r, r^3\}\) is not a subgroup because it does not contain the identity.

    2. We will prove both directions, starting by showing that if \(a \not\in H\) then \(aH\) is not a group. Assume \(a \not\in H\), then \(a^{-1} \not\in H\), and thus there is no element in \(H\) such that \(a*h = e\). Since \(aH\) does not contain the identity, it is not a group.

      Going the other way, we will prove that if \(a \in H\), then \(aH = H\). Certainly \(aH \subseteq H\) because \(H\) is closed so \(a*h \in H\) for all \(a*h \in aH\). However \(H \subseteq aH\) because for each \(h \in H\), we can let \(h' = a^{-1}*h \in H\), and then we see that \(h = a*h' \in aH\).

Choice Exercise 9 [≤6] (Relations)

In class, we defined an equivalence relation as a relation that is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive—but this isn't a very good definition if I don't tell you what a relation is to begin with!

Formally, a relation \(R\) on a set \(X\) is a (perhaps improper) subset of the Cartesian product of sets \(R \subseteq X \times X\), and we say \(x_1 \mathbin{\mathrm{R}} x_2\) if and only if \((x_1,x_2) \in R\).

  1. [1] Let \(X = \{1, 2, 3\}\), and write out the relation "less than" formally as a subset of \(X \times X\).
  2. [2] Determine if the following relations are reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. Explain why each has or does not have each property.
    1. Let \(X\) be subsets of \(\{1, 2, \dots, 5\}\), and let \(R\) be the relation "is a proper subset of".
    2. Let \(X\) be the set of points in the Cartesian plane \(\mathbb R^2\), and let \(R\) be the relation where \(\vec x_1 \mathbin{\mathrm{R}} \vec x_2\) if and only if \(|\vec x_1 - \vec x_2| \leq 1\).
    3. Let \(X\) be the set of triangles in the plane and let \(R\) be the relation "is similar to".
    4. Let \(X\) be the set of positive integers \(R\) be the relation "is a divisor of".

Choice Exercise 11 [4+3+6] (Conjugation and centralizers)

Given \(a,b \in (G,*)\) we say \(a\) is conjugate to \(b\) if there exists some \(g \in G\) such that \(a = g * b * g^{-1}\).

  1. [4] Suppose that we have a square on the ground and you are standing south of it and your friend is standing west of it. You apply \(f \in D_4\) to the square, flipping it horizontally. You ask your friend what symmetry you applied, and your friend replies that you applied \(r^2f \in G\).
    The reason that \(f\) can look like \(r^2f\) from a different perspective is what it means for \(f\) and \(r^2f\) to be conjugate.
    1. Prove \(f\) and \(r^2f\) are conjugate in \(D_4\) by finding an element \(g \in D_4\) such that \(f = g * r^2f * g^{-1}\).
    2. Prove that for all \(g \in D_4\), \(r^2 = g * r^2 * g^{-1}\). It might be useful to use the fact that every element in \(D_4\) can be written as \(r^k\) or \(r^kf\). This means that \(r^2\) is only conjugate to itself.
    3. Determine all elements in \(D_4\) that are conjugate to \(r\).
  2. [3] Prove that "is conjugate to" is an equivalence relation.
  3. [3+1+2] The center of a group \((G,*)\) is denoted \(Z(G)\) and consists of the elements that commute with every element in \(G\) \[ Z(G) = \{ z \in G \mid z = g*z*g^{-1} \forall g \in G \}. \] In terms of conjugation, this can be described as the "subgroup of all elements that are conjugate only to themselves" or the symmetries that look the same from every perspective.
    1. Show that for every group \(G\), the center of the group \(Z(G)\) is a subgroup of \(G\).
    2. Prove that for every group \(G\) the center of the group is an abelian group.
    3. Determine the center of \(D_4\), the dihedral group of the square.
