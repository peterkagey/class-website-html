Exercise 3 [5] (Cayley diagram) You are encouraged to do this problem on paper or a tablet, and include a photo/screenshot following the instructions in HW 1 Exercise 8.10.
  1. Suppose we have a coin with two states (heads and tails) and thus two symmetries (identity and flip). We call this group \(C_2\), the cyclic group of order \(2\). Draw a Cayley diagram for \(C_2\).
  2. Draw a Cayley diagram for \(D_3\) (the dihedral group of the triangle) using the generators \(r\) and \(r^2f\).
  3. Suppose that we have a coin and a triangle on the table, and we consider this as a system. We call this the direct product of \(C_2\) and \(D_3\) and denote it \({C_2 \times D_3}\).
    We describe each symmetries as a pair \((g_1, g_2)\), where \(g_1 \in C_2\) tells us what we do to the coin, and \(g_2\in D_3\) tells us what we do to the triangle. For instance, if \(c\) means "flip the coin", then we have the following symmetries:
    • flip the coin and rotate the triangle by \(120^\circ\), which we denote \((c, r)\); or
    • we could not flip the coin, but flip the triangle horizontally, which we denote \((e_1, f)\); or
    • we flip the coin, but do nothing to the triangle, which we denote \((c, e_2)\); or ...
    1. How many states does this system have? (i.e. how many distinct symmetries does it have?)
    2. Draw a Cayley diagram illustrating how to generate \(C_2 \times D_3\) with the following three symmetries:
      • flip the coin, and do nothing to the triangle \((c, e_2)\),
      • do nothing to the coin, and rotate the triangle \((e_1, r)\),
      • do nothing to the coin, and flip the triangle horizontally \((e_1, f)\).
    3. Can \( C_2 \times D_3 \) be generated with just two generators? If so, state which two and use them to draw a Cayley diagram. If not, why not?
Exercise 4 [5] (Cyclic groups)

Recall that we say a group \(G\) with operation \(\star\) is an abelian group if for every choice of symmetries \(a, b \in G\), \[ a \star b = b \star a. \]

While the cycle group of order \(n\) is specifically \[C_n = \langle r \mid r^n = e\rangle, \] a cyclic group is any group with an element \(g\) (called a generator), such that every element of the group can be written in the form \(g^k\), where \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\) is an integer.

  1. Prove that every cyclic group is abelian.
  2. Draw a Cayley table for the direct product of cyclic groups \(C_2 \times C_3\), where \(C_n = \langle r \mid r^n = e \rangle\).
  3. A group is abelian if and only if it is the same when you reflect its Cayley table across the main diagonal (like a matrix transpose). Explain informally why this is. Is \(C_2 \times C_3\) abelian?
  4. It turns out (but it is not obvious!) that \(C_2 \times C_3\) is cyclic. Find a generator in the group that when applied repeatedly reaches every element in the group and use it to draw a Cayley diagram.
  5. We say that the order of a group is the number of elements in the group and that the order of an element is the number of times you need to apply it to get back to the identity. Thus a group is cyclic precisely when it contains an elements whose order is equal to the order of the group. Check the order of every element in the dihedral group \(D_3\) to prove that this group is not cyclic.
  6. Prove that not every abelian group is cyclic by giving an example of a direct product of cyclic groups that is not itself a cyclic group.

Choice Exercise 6 [≤6] (Symmetric group practice)

Do any subset of the following for 1 point each:

  1. Let $\pi = (1\ 5\ 3\ 8\ 4\ 6) \in S_9$, explain why $|\pi| = 6,$ where $|\pi|$ is the order of the element $\pi$. Moreover, explain how you know that if a cycle has length $k$ then it has order $k$.
  2. Let $\pi' = (1\ 5)(3\ 8\ 4) \in S_9$, prove that the order of $\pi' = 6$.
  3. Look up what it means for two cycles to be disjoint. In particular, are $(1\ 5\ 9\ 7)$ and $(3\ 8\ 4)$ disjoint? Are $(1\ 5\ 9\ 8)$ and $(3\ 8\ 4)$ disjoint?
  4. Explain why \[(1\ 5\ 9\ 7)(3\ 8\ 4) = (3\ 8\ 4)(1\ 5\ 9\ 7),\] but \[(1\ 5\ 9\ 8)(3\ 8\ 4) \neq (3\ 8\ 4)(1\ 5\ 9\ 8).\] Next, explain how you know that \[ \begin{align} \left((1\ 5\ 9\ 7)(3\ 8\ 4)\right)^7 &= (1\ 5\ 9\ 7)^7(3\ 8\ 4)^7 \\ &= (1\ 5\ 9\ 7)^{3}(3\ 8\ 4)^1 \\ &= (1\ 7\ 9\ 5)(3\ 8\ 4). \end{align} \]
  5. Give an example of a $3$-cycle and a $4$-cycle whose product is a $2$-cycle.
  6. Suppose that we write $\sigma = c_1 c_2 \dots c_n$ as a product of disjoint cycles. Prove that the order of sigma $|\sigma| \leq \operatorname{LCM}(|c_1|,|c_2|,\dots,|c_n|)$, where $|c_i|$ is the order (equivalently length) of the cycle, and use this to compute the order of \[ \tau = (8\ 2)(1\ 9\ 4)(3\ 7\ 5\ 6) \in S_9. \]

Choice Exercise 9 [≤9] (Hypersphere symmetries)

The group of symmetries of the \(n\)-dimensional sphere is called the orthogonal group \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\). Formally, \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\) is the set of \(n\times n\) matrices whose columns are orthonormal vectors, meaning that each column viewed as a vector has magnitude \(1\) and the dot product of any two distinct columns is zero.

  1. [2] Show that a matrix \(A \in O(n,\mathbb{R})\) if and only if $A^T A = AA^T = I_n$.
  2. Show that for all integers $n \geq 1$...
    1. [1] The identity matrix is in \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\)
    2. [1] If \(A \in O(n,\mathbb{R})\), then \(A\) is invertible and \(A^{-1} \in O(n,\mathbb{R})\)
  3. [1] Use the facts that \(\det(A) = \det(A^T)\) and that \(\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)\) to prove that if \(A \in O(n,\mathbb{R})\), then \(\det(A) = \pm1\).
  4. [2] The special orthogonal group \(SO(n,\mathbb{R})\) is the subset of \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\) consisting of the in matrices in \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\) whose determinant is positive. Prove that this is a group. (This is the group of rotations of the unit hypersphere in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)).
  5. [2] Give an example of an element of \(O(n,\mathbb{R})\) that sends the vector \[ \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}^T \mapsto \begin{pmatrix}1/6 & 1/3 & 1/6\end{pmatrix}^T. \]
