Choice Exercise 6 [≤6] (Quiz review) Look over the solutions to the week 5 and week 6 quizzes. For each quiz, write a sentence or two about a mistake or misconception you had, and clarify how to fix it.
  1. The quiz from Tuesday of week 5 asked you about a binary operation on cosets being well-defined.
    Answer key.
    1. Let \(a = r^2\), \(a' = r^2f\), and \(b = b' = r\). Then \((r^2 * r)H = H \neq (r^2f * r) H = rfH \), since \(rfH = \{rf, r\} \neq H\).
    2. If \(G = D_4\), then \(r^2\) commutes with the generators, since \(f * r^2 = r^2f \) and \(r * r^2 = r^2 * r \), and therefore \(r^2\) commutes with all of \(G\). Since \(e\) also commutes with all of the elements of \(G\), we can see that everything in \(N = \{e, r^2\} \) commutes with each element of \(G\).
      If \(a' = a * n_1\) and \(b' = b * n_2\) for some \(n_1, n_2 \in N\) then \[ a' * b' = (a * n_1 * b * n_2) = (a * b * n_1 * n_2) \in (a * b)N. \]
  2. The quiz from Thursday of week 5, which was given to the morning section on Tuesday of week 6 asked you to show that a subgroup is not normal or to show that \(\langle r^n \rangle \triangleleft D_{2n}\).
    Answer key.
    1. We can see that \(H = \langle r^2f \rangle = \{e, r^2f\}\) is not normal in \(D_3\) by conjugating \(r^2f\) by \(r\) \[ r * r^2f * r^{-1} = fr^{-1} = rf \not\in H. \]
    2. In \(D_{2n}\), we can check that \(r^n\) is in its own conjugacy class because \(r^nf = fr^{2n-n} = fr^n\), and thus \[ \begin{align*} r^k * r^n * r^{-k} &= r^{k+n-k} \\ &= r^n, \\ r^kf * r^n * (r^kf)^{-1} &= r^{n+k}f * r^kf \\ &= r^{n+k}f*fr^{2n-k} \\ &= r^n. \end{align*} \]
  3. The quiz from Thursday of week 6 asked you to construct an isomorphism \(\phi\colon D_2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times C_2\).
    Answer key.

    In this particular case, any choice works, as long as you send the identity to the identity. \[ \begin{align*} \phi(e) &= (0, e)\\ \phi(r) &= (1, e)\\ \phi(f) &= (1, r)\\ \phi(rf) &= (0, r). \end{align*} \] This is clearly a bijection, and we check that it is structure preserving by checking the relations on \(D_2\) are preserved: \[ \begin{align*} \phi(r)\star\phi(r) &= (1, e) \star (1, e) = (1 + 1, e * e) = (0, e) \\ \phi(f)\star\phi(f) &= (1, r) \star (1, r) = (1 + 1, r * r) = (0, e) \\ (\phi(r)\star\phi(f))^2 &= ((1, e) \star (1, r))^2 = (0, r)^2 = (0, e). \\ \end{align*} \]

  1. [≤2] Explain briefly and informally how you know that \[G = \{e^x \mid x \in \mathbb{R} \}\] forms a group under multiplication, and show that it is isomorphic to \((\mathbb{R},+)\) where \(\phi\) is the natural logarithm \(\underbrace{\ln}_\phi\colon G \to (\mathbb{R},+)\).
  2. [≤6] Prove the following statements about structure-preserving maps from the group \((G,*)\) to \((H, \star)\). Recall a map \(\phi \colon G \to H\) is structure-preserving if \[\phi(g_1 * g_2) = \phi(g_1) \star \phi(g_2)\] for all \(g_1, g_2 \in G\).
    1. [1] If \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is a structure-preserving map, then \( \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1}. \)
    2. [3] Use induction to prove that if \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is a structure-preserving map, then \( \phi(g^n) = \phi(g)^n \) for ...
      1. \(n = 0\)
      2. all integers \(n > 0\)
      3. all integers \(n < 0\)
    3. [2] Prove that if \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is an isomorphism, then \(G\) is abelian if and only if \(H\) is abelian. (Hint: use the fact that if \(\phi\) is an isomorphism, then every \(h \in H\) can be written as \(\phi(g) = h\) for some \(g \in G\).)
  3. [3] Let $G = D_{12}$ and let $N = \langle r^4, rf \rangle$.

    3. Prove that \(G/N\) is isomorphic to either \(G' = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}\) or \(G' = C_2 \times C_2\) by giving an explicit bijection \(\phi\colon G/N \to G'\) and drawing the Cayley table for \(G'\), and seeing that it matches the Cayley table you found earlier.

Choice Exercise 10 [≤6] (Changing perspective on conjugation)
  1. [3] Conjugating matrices. In linear algebra matrix conjugation (\(BAB^{-1}\)) allows us to "change perspective" by changing basis. That is, if you are thinking of a linear transformation \(A\) with respect to a given basis, and I am thinking about the same linear transformation but in another basis related by \(B\), we can "translate" between our perspectives with (matrix) conjugation: \(A' = BAB^{-1}\).
    Give an example of how this works (perhaps by watching a YouTube video or reading a textbook that discusses this), and write a few sentences explaining what's going on in your own words.
  2. [3] Conjugating permutations. Suppose that we're thinking about $S_n$, and we're scrambling $n$ objects, but the objects are randomly positioned on the table. Reasonable minds could disagree about which position is position $1$ versus position $2$ versus position $n$. You come up with some labeling and I come up with some labeling too, and our labelings are related by the permutation \(\sigma\), which tells that what you are calling position \(1\), I am calling position \(\sigma(1)\).
    Use this way of thinking to find \(\sigma \in S_7\) such that \[ \sigma (314)(25)(67) \sigma^{-1} = (124)(65)(37), \] and explain formally or informally why conjugating permutations preserves cycle structure (e.g., in the above example the conjugates both consist of one \(3\)-cycle and two \(2\)-cycles.)
Choice Exercise 11 [≤11] ⭐️ (Programming Bézout's identity) The programming part of this problem occurs in part 5. Parts 1–4 will be graded normally, and part 5 is graded based on spending 30 minutes attempting to code it in a programming language of your choice.

An earlier problem suggested that if $\gcd(n,k) = 1$ then $\langle r^k \rangle = C_n$. In this problem you will prove that this (and other group theory facts) follow from Bézout's identity, which states that if $k$ and $n$ are coprime (i.e., $\gcd(n,k)=1$), then there exist integers $a$ and $b$ such that $ak + bn = 1$. At the end, you will prove Bézout's identity.

  1. [1] Assuming Bézout's identity, explain how $r^{ak}=r$ and thus $\langle r^k \rangle = C_n$.
  2. [2] Again assuming Bézout's identity, give an explicit element of ${\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}}$ that has order $kn$, and use it to construct an isomorphism \[ \phi\colon \mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/kn\mathbb{Z}. \]
  3. [2] Show how the above isomorphism gives a (non-constructive) proof of the existence part of the Chinese Remainder Theorem which states that if $n$ and $k$ are coprime, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are any integers, then there exists an integer $m$ such that \[\begin{align*} m &\equiv \alpha \pmod n \\ m &\equiv \beta \pmod k. \end{align*}\]
  4. [4] Now we will prove Bézout's identity in the case that $\gcd(n,k) = 1$.
    1. We'll start by describing the basis of the Euclidean algorithm. Suppose without loss of generality that $n \geq k$ (since $\gcd(n,k) = \gcd(n,k)$), and show that $\gcd(n,k) = \gcd(n-k,k)$.
    2. Show that we can only continue this process for a finite number of steps before we reach $\gcd(1,1) = 1$.
    3. Now prove that we can inductively unwind the whole process in the following sense: if we can find integers $c_1$ and $c_2$ such that $c_1(n-k) + c_2k = 1$, then we can find integers $d_1$ and $d_2$ such that $d_1 n + d_2 k = 1$.
    4. Show that it works in practice by computing $\gcd(7,4) = 1$ using a series of moves like those described in part a, and unwind it to find integers $a$ and $b$ such that $7a + 4b = 1$. (An intermediate step might be that $4c_1 + 3c_2 = 1$ when $c_1 = -2$ and $c_2 = 3$.)
  5. [3] Write a program that takes in two positive integers $(n,k)$ and returns a pair $(a,b)$ such that $ak + bn = \gcd(n,k)$.
