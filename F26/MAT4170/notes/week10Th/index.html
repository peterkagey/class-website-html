Integral domains

Today we will discuss integral domains, which is an idea at the heart of algebraic number theory.

Integral domain

A commutative ring \((R,+,\times)\) that is not the zero ring is called a integral domain if for all nonzero \(a, b \in R\), \(a \times b \neq 0_R.\)

Arguably the most important integral domain is the integers \(\mathbb{Z}\). We will also see that if we build a polynomial ring using an integral domain as the ring of coefficients, then the polynomial ring is also an integral domain—thus \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) is an integral domain.

Additionally all fields are integral domains, so \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{C}\) are integral domains. Also, when \(p\) is a prime number, then we saw that \(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\) is a field, and thus it is also an integral domain.

However, we've also seen examples of rings that are not integral domains. Perhaps the easiest example of this is the ring of integers modulo a composite number, such as \(\mathbb{Z}_4\), where \(2 \times 2 = 0\).

Now we will state and prove the earlier claim about polynomial rings.

If \(R\) is an integral domain, then the ring of polynomials \(R[x]\) is also an integral domain.
Suppose that we have two nonzero polynomials, \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) with (nonzero) leading coefficients \(r_n\) and \(r'_n\) respectively. \begin{align} p_1 &= r_n x^n + r_1x + r_0 \\ p_2 &= r'_m x^m + r'_1x + r'_0 \end{align} Then the leading coefficient of \(p_1 \times p_2\) is \(r_nr'_m \neq 0\) because \(R\) is an integral domain, and so the product of the polynomials is not the zero polynomial.
