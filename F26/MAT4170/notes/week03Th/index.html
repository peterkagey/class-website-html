Subgroups

A common theme in mathematics is that in order to understand a complicated object, we try to break it down into smaller pieces that we can understand individually. This is part of the reason that we are interested in prime factorizations of integers: knowing the pieces can help us understand the whole.

One of the most powerful techniques we have for understanding groups is understanding their subgroups, which are subsets of the group are themselves groups.

Perhaps the most immediate subgroup is the copy of the cyclic group \(C_n\) which lives inside of a dihedral group, which consists of the subset of rotations of \(D_n\), we get a group that is essentially the same as \(C_n\).
A subgroup of a group \(G, \star\) is a subset of \(H \subseteq G\) which forms a group under \(\star\). We denote that \(H\) is a subset of \(G\) by writing \(H \leq G\).

In the same way that we often describe a group in terms of a group presentation consisting of relations and generators, we often describe the subgroups just in terms of generators (which are subject to the same relations as the whole group).

Given a group \(G\) and elements \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n \in G\) called generators. We call \[ \langle g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n \rangle \] the subgroup generated by \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\), and it consists of the set of all words that can be formed out of the generators and their inverses.
  1. Let \(G_1 = D_4\). Compute all of the elements in the subgroup \(H = \langle r^2, f \rangle \leq G_1\), and determine whether \(H\) is cyclic. Is \(H\) an abelian group?
  2. Let \(G_2 = (\mathbb{Z},+)\). Describe all of the elements in the subgroup \(N = \langle 9, 19\rangle \leq G_2\), and determine whether \(N\) is cyclic. Is \(N\) an abelian group?

Modular arithmetic

We've seen several times in this class that we often only care about something's remainder on division. For example, we compute \(r^{919} \in C_5\) by noticing that \({919 \div 5 = 183\ \mathrm{r}. 4}\), and thus \(r^{919} = (r^5)^{183}r^4 = e^{183}r^4 = r^4.\)

When we only care about the remainder on division, we like to use modular arithmetic, which is sometimes introduced as "clock arithmetic", since we don't care about the number of hours since the start of the year, we only care about the number of hours modulo \(12\).

We say that \(a\) is congruent to \(b\) modulo \(n\), written \[ a \equiv b \pmod n, \] if \(a - b = kn\) for some integer \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\).
If my (July) birthday this year was on a Sunday, and next year is not a leap year, then my next birthday is 365 days after the previous one. Since \[365 \equiv 1 \pmod 7,\] this means that next year's birthday will fall on a Monday.
Prove that if \(a \equiv a' \pmod n\) and \(b \equiv b' \pmod n\), then \[ a + b \equiv a' + b' \pmod n. \]
Let \(\mathcal U_{12}\) be the set of integers \(0 \leq n < 12\) that have a multiplicative inverse modulo \(12\), that is, numbers \(n\) such that there exists some integer \(k\) with \(nk \equiv 1 \pmod{12}\). Show that \(\mathcal U_{12}\) is a group under multiplication modulo \(12\).

Isomorphism

Often it seems two groups are somehow "essentially the same," we make this precise with the notion of isomorphism

We argue informally that \(D_3\) and \(S_3\) are isomorphic by labeling an "upright" equilateral triangle with the top vertex labeled \(1\), the bottom-left labeled \(2\), and the bottom-right labeled \(3\).

Then we can see that \(r \in D_3\) moves the ball in position \(1\) to position \(2\), the ball in position \(2\) to position \(3\), and the ball in position \(3\) to position \(1\). We would describe this permutation as \((123) \in S_3\). Moreover, we can see that \(f \in D_3\) behaves in exactly the same way as \((23) \in S_3\).

The six elements of \(D_3\) correspond in this way to the six elements of \(S_3\) (that is, they form a bijection) and moreover, they follow the same relations \[ (123)^3 = (23)^2 = \left((123)\circ(23)\right)^2 = e, \] a property that we call structure-preserving. We call these a "structure-preserving bijections" an isomorphism, and we will define this more formally in a few weeks.

One way of showing that two groups are isomorphic is by drawing Cayley diagrams for each that are identical if we replace the "states" with unlabeled vertices and remove specific labels for the edges (however, we must keep continue to keep track of the colors of the edges).

On your homework, you showed that both \(C_6\) and the direct product \(C_2 \times C_3\) are cyclic groups of order \(6\). Later we will see that this means that they are isomorphic.

Consider the subgroup \(\langle r_6^2, f_6 \rangle \leq D_6\). Give a bijection between this and \(D_3 = \langle r_3, f_3 \mid r_3^3 = f_3^2 = (r_3f_3)^2 = e \rangle\) such that corresponding elements in the subgroup \(\langle r_6^2, f_6 \rangle\) satisfy the relations in \(D_3\).
