Group actions

For the last few lectures of the term, we'll be thinking about group theory—albeit with fewer layers of abstraction than when we left off with the first isomorphism theorem.

At some level, the reason we care about groups is because of what they do to other mathematical objects, and in this sense the idea of group actions links group theory to symmetry. It will also gives us a sense of why sometimes we read left-to-right and sometimes we read right-to-left.

Group actions

Part of the reason that group actions can be a tricky topic for students to learn is that groups and group actions are usually conflated when students are first learning the topic.

With a group action we have a group \(G\) and a set of objects \(X\) and a group element \(g \in G\) takes a set element \(x \in X\) and sends it to a new set element \(x' \in X\). We typically denote the action with a dot, writing \(g \cdot x = x'\) or \(x \cdot g = x'\), depending on what convention we're using.

We have a group \(G\) and a set of objects \(X\) and a group element \(g \in G\) takes a set element \(x \in X\) and sends it to a new set element \(g \cdot x = x' \in X\).

The most familiar example is that we let \(G = S_4\) the symmetric group on four objects. If \(\pi = (142)\), then we might define a "function application" group action on the set \(X = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}\) which treats \(\pi\) like the bijection that it is, sending \(\pi \cdot 4 = \pi(4) = 2\) and sending \(\pi \cdot 3 = \pi(3) = 3\).

This example is useful, but it is so close to the way that we were already thinking about \(S_4\) as a group, that it can give students the wrong idea. Here's a slightly more novel example.

Label the vertices of a hexagon with \(X = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}\), and we will let the dihedral group of the triangle \(G=D_3\) act on it (on the right!) following our usual conventions that \(r \in D_3\) corresponds to a \(120^\circ\) positive rotation and \(f\) corresponds to a horizontal (left-to-right) flip.

See that \(2 \cdot f = 1\), because a horizontal flip moves the vertex labeled \(2\) to the vertex labeled \(1\). Similarly, \(3 \cdot r = 5\) because if we start at \(3\) and apply a \(+120^\circ\) rotation, we end up at \(5\).

Now we will see the definitions of right and left group actions. Note that when we read \(r * f \in D_n\) left-to-right, that's because we're thinking of acting on the triangle on the right, and when we read \((12) \circ (143) \in S_n\) right-to-left, that's because we're thinking of permutations as acting on the objects on the left.

Let \((G,*)\) be a group, and let \(X\) be a set. A left group action is a function \(\cdot\colon G \times X \to X\) such that
  1. for all \(x\in X\), \(e \cdot x = x\), and
  2. for all \(g_1, g_2 \in G\) and \(x\in X\), \[ g_2 \cdot (g_1 \cdot x) = (g_2 * g_1) \cdot x. \]

With the above definition in mind, you can anticipate the following.

Let \((G,*)\) be a group, and let \(X\) be a set. A right group action is a function \(\cdot\colon X \times G \to X\) such that
  1. for all \(x\in X\), \(x \cdot e = x\), and
  2. for all \(g_1, g_2 \in G\) and \(x\in X\), \[ (x \cdot g_1) \cdot g_2 = x \cdot (g_1 * g_2). \]
