Fields

If rings are structures with operations that behave like addition, subtraction (additive inverses), and multiplication, then fields also bring division (multiplicative inverses) into the mix. We also require that multiplication is commutative.

A field is a commutative ring \(F\) with at least two elements such that for every element \(a \in F \setminus \{0_F\}\), \(a\) has a multiplicative inverse denoted \(a^{-1}\).
The most familiar examples of fields are the rational numbers \(\mathbb{Q}\), the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\), and the complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}\). Recall that in linear algebra, we define a vector space over a field, and so we often deal with real-valued or complex-valued matrices. Many of the results that you've seen in a linear algebra class also work over an arbitrary field \(\mathbb{F}\).

Not all fields are infinite! Let \(\mathbb{Z}_n = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\) denote the integers mod \(n\). Show that \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) and \(\mathbb{Z}_3\) are fields, but \(\mathbb{Z}_4\) is not a field.

You can generalize this further: show that for \(n \geq 2\), \(\mathbb{Z}_n\) is a field if and only if \(n\) is prime.
