Homomorphism

Today we will discuss group homomorphisms, which are just group isomorphisms where we relax the requirement that the maps are bijections—we just need the maps to be structure-preserving.

Let \((G,*)\) and \((H,\star)\) and be groups. A group homomorphism is a map \(\phi\colon G \to H\) such that for all \(g_1,g_2 \in G\), \[ \phi(g_1 * g_2) = \phi(g_1) \star \phi(g_2). \]

We've actually seen a group homomorphism on the homework already in HW 4, Choice Exercise 10, which had students embed the dihedral group \(D_4\) in \(S_4\) by describing each rotation/reflection as a permutation in cycle notation.

We can embed the dihedral group $D_n$ into $S_n$. For example, if we mark the corners of the square $1$, $2$, $3$, and $4$, then rotating/reflecting the square permutes these numbers.

  1. Write down (in cycle notation) the permutations \(\pi\) and \(\sigma\) in \(S_4\) that correspond to \(r\) and \(f \in D_4\) respectively. (i.e., \(\phi(r) = \pi\) and \(\phi(f) = \sigma\).) Show that these permutations satisfy the group relations on \(D_4\).
  2. Show that you can find an analogous embedding of \(D_n\) in \(S_n\) for all \(n).
  3. You can actually embed \(D_6\) in \(S_5\)! Can you figure out how?

While the above is an example of an injective (or 1-to-1) homomorphism, we don't actually need our homomorphisms to be injective!

Let \((G,*)\) and \((H,\star)\) and be groups. There always exists a group homomorphism between \(G\) and \(H\).
Let \(\phi\colon G \to H\) be the map that sends \(\phi(g) = e_H\) for all \(g \in G\). Then \[ \phi(g_1 * g_2) = e_H \] and \[ \phi(g_1 * g_2) = \phi(g_1) \star \phi(g_2) = e_H \star e_H = e_H, \] so the map is structure-preserving. (This map is called the trivial homomorphism and is not a very interesting example of a homomorphism.)

Kernels of homomorphisms

Often we care about precisely how a homomorphism fails to be injective, which we capture with the idea of a kernel.

Given a group homomorphism \(\phi\colon G \to H\), we say that the kernel of the homomorphism is the subset of elements of \(G\) that map to the identity in \(H\): \[ \ker(\phi) = \left\{ g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H \right\}. \]

In the first example (embedding \(D_4\) into \(S_4\)), the kernel is trivial: \[\ker(\phi) = \{e\} \subseteq D_4.\] In the above proposition/proof about the trivial homomorphism, the kernel is all of the domain: \[ \ker(\phi) = G. \] The kernel can be between these two extremes too. There is a homomorphism \(\phi\colon D_6 \to D_3\) which satisfies \(\phi(r_6) = r_3\) and \(\phi(f_6)=f_3\). It sends \(\phi(r_6^3) = r_3^3 = e\), and its kernel is \[ \ker(\phi) = \{e, r_6^3\}. \]

Prove that for every group homomorphism \(\phi\colon G \to H\), the kernel \(\ker(\phi)\) is a normal subgroup of \(G\).
(Hint: first prove \(\ker(\phi) \leq G\), and then prove \(\ker(\phi) \triangleleft G\).)
