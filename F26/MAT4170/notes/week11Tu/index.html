Algebraic numbers

Today we will discuss the degree of a polynomial in \(R[x]\), the idea of algebraic and transcendental numbers, and the notion of algebraic degree of an algebraic number.

Degrees of polynomials and algebraic numbers

We say that the degree of a univariate polynomial in \(R[x]\) is the highest exponent occurring in the polynomial, where the degree of the zero polynomial is undefined.

When we have a multivariate polynomial \(R[x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n]\), we say that the degree of the polynomial is the highest sum of exponents of a given term. So the degree of \(4x^2y-3x^4y^2\) is \(6\).

We define an algebraic number as a complex number that is the root of a univariate polynomial with integer (equivalently rational) coefficients. We say a transcendental number is a complex number that is not the root of a univariate polynomial.

For instance, \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) is algebraic because it is the root of the polynomial \(x^3 - 2\), but \(\pi = 3.1415\dots\) is not algebraic, because it is not the root of any polynomial with integer coefficients.

The algebraic degree of an algebraic number \(\alpha\) is the smallest degree of a polynomial that has \(\alpha\) as a root.

For example, the algebraic degree of \(\sqrt[3]{2}\) is \(3\) because it is not the root of any linear or quadratic polynomial with integer coefficients, but it is a root of a degree-\(3\) polynomial, as shown above.

Note that \(\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is a root of the degree-\(4\) polynomial \(x^4-10 x^2+1\), and so the algebraic degree of \(\alpha\) is at most \(4\).
Note that \(\alpha^2 = 2 \sqrt{6}+5\) and \(\alpha^3 = 11 \sqrt{2}+9 \sqrt{3}\). Prove that there are no integer linear combinations of \(1\), \(a\), \(\alpha^2\), and \(\alpha^3\) that equal zero. That is, for all integers \(b_0, b_1, b_2, b_3 \in \mathbb{Z}\) prove that \[ b_0 + b_1\alpha + b_2\alpha^2 + b_3\alpha^3 \neq 0. \]
