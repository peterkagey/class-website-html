Burnside's lemma

Orbits

An orbit consists of all of the elements of a set that are related by a group action.

Let \(G\) be a group that acts on a set \(X\) on the right. We say that the orbit of \(x \in X\) is the set of elements \[ x \cdot G = \{ x \cdot g \mid g \in G \}. \]

Later, we will prove that orbits are an equivalence relation, and thus we can partition our set into orbits. This is precisely what is counted by Burnside's lemma.

The set partition of \(X\) into orbits under \(G\) is denoted \(X/G\).

Next we will prove that orbits are equivalence classes and thus partition the set \(X\).

Let \(G\) be a group that acts on a set \(X\). Let \(\sim\) denote relation on \(X\) where \({x \sim y}\) if \(x \cdot g = y\) for some \(g \in G\).
We check the three conditions of an equivalence relation.
Reflexive.
For all \(x \in X\), \(x \sim x\) because \(x \cdot e = x\).
Symmetric.
Suppose that \(x \sim y\), meaning that there exists \(g \in G\) with \(x \cdot g = y\). Then \[ y \cdot g^{-1} = (x \cdot g) \cdot g^{-1} = x \cdot (g * g^{-1}) = x \cdot e = x, \] so \(y \sim x\).
Transitive.
Suppose that \(x \sim y\) and \(y \sim z\) with \(x \cdot g_1 = y\) and \(y \cdot g_2 = z\). Then \[ x \cdot (g_1 * g_2) = (x \cdot g_1) \cdot g_2 = y \cdot g_2 = z, \] so \(x \sim z\).
Therefore \(\sim\) is an equivalence relation, and the equivalence classes are orbits.

Counting orbits

We will present Burnside's lemma without proof. Again, if you want to see a proof sketch for Burnside's Lemma, I encourage you to check out the video in Extra Credit Exercise 7 on HW X.

Because directly counting objects up to symmetry is hard, in general, we use Burnside's lemma which allows us to do a trick: count the number of objects that are fixed (look the same) under each symmetry, and average these numbers. Burnside's lemma tells us that this gives us the number that we're looking for.

Let \(G\) be a finite group that acts on a set \(X\). For each \(g \in G\), let \[X^g = \{ x \in X \mid g \cdot x = x \}\] denote the set of elements of \(X\) that are fixed under \(g\). Then set of orbits of elements in \(X\) under \(G\) is given by the sum \[ |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|}\big(\sum_{g \in G} |X^g| \big). \]

This is a theorem that is meant to be applied! So let's count some objects up to symmetry!

We will use Burnside's lemma to count the four distinct tilings shown in the first exercise. We begin by computing \(|X^e|\), the number of tilings that are fixed under the identity ("do nothing") symmetry. This is just the number of possible tilings, and since we have two choices for each of the four cells, we see that \(|X^e| = 2^4 = 16\).

Next we count \(|X^r| = |X^{r^3}|\), the number of tilings that are fixed under \(r\) (or equivalently, fixed under \(r^3\), why?). Notice that we can pick whatever we would like for the first cell, and that choice determines how we fill in the other three cells. Thus \(|X^r| = |X^{r^3}| = 2,\) as shown below.

Next, we compute \(|X^{r^2}| = 2^2 = 4\), which follows from a similar argument as above. We can choose whatever we would like for the top two cells, as long as the bottom two are chosen to be a \(180^\circ\) rotation of the top.

Similarly, we compute \(|X^{f}| = |X^{r^2f}| = 2^2 = 4\). Since \(f\) represents a horizontal reflection, we are free to choose the two tiles for the left two cells, as long as we put their mirror images on the right.

Lastly, we see that \(|X^{rf}| = |X^{r^3f}| = 0\). Since \(r^3f\) corresponds to a flip over the diagonal, whatever pattern we put in the upper-right cell looks different under a flip, thus diagonal flips cannot fix any tilings in \(X\).

Now we use Burnside's lemma, which tells us that the number of tilings of the \(2 \times 2\) grid with these tiles is given by \[ |X/G| = \frac{1}{|G|}\big(\sum_{g \in G} |X^g| \big). \] Recalling that \(G = D_4\) and using the values from earlier, we compute the sum as \[ \frac{1}{8}\big(\underbrace{|X^e|}_{16} + \underbrace{|X^r|}_2 + \underbrace{|X^{r^2}|}_4 + \underbrace{|X^{r^3}|}_2 + \underbrace{|X^{f}|}_4 + \underbrace{|X^{rf}|}_0 + \underbrace{|X^{r^2f}|}_4 + \underbrace{|X^{r^3f}|}_0\big), \] and thus there are \(|X/G| = 4\) tilings of the \(2 \times 2\) grid up to dihedral symmetry.

You may have noticed that we grouped together conjugate elements when we did our counting. In the example above, it may feel intuitive enough to do this, but it's worth seeing why this strategy works in general.

If \(h\) and \(h'\) are conjugate, meaning that \(h' = g * h * g^{-1}\) for some \(g \in G\), then \(|X^h| = |X^{h'}|\).

We will form a bijection between the elements of \(X^h\) and \(X^{h'}\). Suppose that \(h' = g_0 * h * g_0^{-1}\) and \(x \in X^h\), meaning that \(h \cdot x = x\).

Solving for \(h\) yields \(h = g_0^{-1} * h' * g_0\), and thus \[ (g_0^{-1} * h' * g_0) \cdot x = x, \] so applying the action of \(g_0\) on both sides yields \begin{align*} g_0 \cdot \left((g_0^{-1} * h' * g_0) \cdot x\right) &= g_0 \cdot x \\ (g_0 * g_0^{-1}) \cdot \left((h' * g_0) \cdot x\right) &= g_0 \cdot x \\ h' \cdot (g_0 \cdot x) &= g_0 \cdot x, \end{align*} and thus for each \(x \in X^h\), we see that \(g_0 \cdot x \in X^{h'}\).

Notice that the map \(f\colon X^h \to X^{h'}\) which sends \(f(x) = g_0 \cdot x\) is a bijection with inverse map \({f^{-1}(x') = g_0^{-1} \cdot x'}\).
