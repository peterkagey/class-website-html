Required Exercises

Exercise 4 [9] (Rings and fields)
  1. Let \(\mathbb{Z}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\), and recall that in HW 6, Exercise 4.2a, you showed that when \(p\) is a prime, every nonzero element in \((\mathbb{Z}_p,+)\) generates the group.
    1. Explain why this shows that for all nonzero elements \(a \in \mathbb{Z}_p\), there exists some \(1 \leq b < n\) such that \(a^b = 1\).
    2. Show why the above argument implies that in the ring \((\mathbb{Z}_p,+,\times)\), all nonzero elements have a multiplicative inverse, and thus forms a field.
    3. Show that if \(n \geq 2\) is not prime, then \((\mathbb{Z}_n,+,\times)\) is not a field.
  2. Recall that an upper triangular matrix in \(\mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is a matrix where the entry \(a_{ij} = 0\) whenever \(i > j\).
    1. Use the definition of an upper triangular matrix to prove that the sum of two upper triangular matrices is upper triangular.
    2. Write an argument (as formally or informally as you'd like) that convinces yourself that upper triangular matrices form an abelian group under addition.
    3. Recall the definition of matrix multiplication. If \( A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \) with entries \( a_{ij} \) and \( b_{ij} \) respectively then \( AB = C \) has entries \[ c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik} b{kj}. \] Use this definition to prove that if \(A\) and \(B\) are upper triangular matrices then \(C = AB\) is also an upper triangular matrix.
    4. Upper triangular matrices form a ring under matrix addition and matrix multiplication, but they do not form a field. Why not?

Choice Exercise 5 [12] (Ring fundamentals)
  1. [2] Look over the solutions to the week 9 quiz, write a sentence or two about a mistake or misconception you had, and clarify how to fix it.
    The quiz from Thursday of week 9 asked you to prove that in a ring \((R, +, \times)\), \( -a \times -b = a \times b. \)
    Answer key.

    The proposition states that \(-(a \times b) = -a \times b.\) We will use this to that \(a \times b\) and \(-a \times -b\) have the same additive inverse, and thus are equal: \begin{align*} (-a \times -b) + -(a \times b) &= (-a \times -b) + (-a \times b) \\ &= -a \times (-b + b) \\ &= -a \times 0_R \\ &= 0_R. \end{align*} Adding \(a \times b\) to the first and last expression in the above chain of equations yields \begin{align*} -a \times -b = a \times b. \end{align*}

  2. [1] Prove that if \(R\) is a ring and \(r \in R\) satisfies \(r^2 = 1_R\), then either \(r = 1_R\) or \(r = -1_R\).
    (Hint: compute \((1-r)\times(1+r)\).)
  3. [1] Give an example of a non-commutative ring \(R\) and an element \(r \in R\) such that \(r \neq 0_R\), but \(r^2 = 0_R\).
  4. [1] Give a ring \(R\) that has three distinct elements \(r_1, r_2, r_3 \in R\) such that \(r_1^2 = r_1\), \(r_2^2 = r_2\), and \(r_3^2 = r_3\).
  5. [5] The zero ring, denoted \(\mathbf{0} = {0}\) consists of a one-element set.
    1. [1] Like the name suggests, the zero ring is a ring, meaning it has a multiplicative identity. Determine \(1_R \in \mathbf{0}\).
    2. [2] Recall that a commutative ring \((R,+,\times)\) is called a field if \((R \setminus \{0_R\}, \times)\) is an (abelian) group. Prove that if \(R = \mathbf{0}\) then every element in \(R \setminus \{0_R\}\) has a multiplicative inverse, but nevertheless, \(R\) is not a field.
      (Hint: this has a MAT 3100W flavor of logic.)
    3. [2] Prove that if \(R\) is a ring, then \(0_R = 1_R\) if and only if \(R = \mathbf{0}\).
  6. [2] There is a mathematical object called a rng, which satisfies the ring axioms, except for that it doesn't need a multiplicative identity. (No \(i\), get it?) Give an example of a rng that is not a ring, and prove that it is a rng.
  7. [3] Prove that \[ \mathbb{Q}[\sqrt{3}] = \{ a + b\sqrt{2} \mid a, b \in \mathbb{Q} \} \subseteq \mathbb{R} \] forms a field under ordinary addition and multiplication.
Choice Exercise 6 [5+5] (Associativity and distributivity in \(\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and \(\mathbb{C}\))

  1. Given two matrices $A, B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ with entries $\{a_{ij}\}$ and $\{b_{ij}\}$ respectively, we define matrix multiplication as $AB = C \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ such that the entries of \(C\) are given by \(c_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}.\)

    1. [2] (This was also Exercise 8.2b on HW0.)
      Use the definition of matrix multiplication to prove that matrix multiplication is associative: for all \( n \times n \) matrices \( A \), \( B \) and \( C \), \[ (AB)C = A(BC).\]
    2. [3] Use the definition of matrix multiplication to prove that matrix multiplication (left) distributes over matrix addition: for all \( n \times n \) matrices \(A, B, C\), \[ A(B + C) = AB + AC. \]
  2. Recall that in the ring of complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}, + , \times\), we define multiplication such that \[ (a_1 + b_1i) \times (a_2 + b_2i) = (a_1a_2 - b_1b_2) + (a_1b_2 + a_2b_1)i. \]
    1. [2] Prove that complex multiplication is associative.
    2. [1] Prove that complex multiplication is commutative.
    3. [2] Prove that complex multiplication distributes over addition. (You only need to check one direction because of commutativity.)
Choice Exercise 7 [7] (Vector spaces over a finite field)

It turns out that many (but not all) of the theorems that you learned about vector spaces over \(\mathbb{R}\) in linear algebra also work over finite fields.

  1. Let \(\mathbb{Z}_5^{3 \times 3}\) be the ring of \(3 \times 3\) matrices over the field \(\mathbb{Z}_5\). Compute the determinant \(\det(A) \in \mathbb{Z}_5\) where \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]
  2. Because the determinant of \(A\) is nonzero, the matrix is invertible. Adapt the standard matrix inversion techniques to compute the inverse matrix \(A^{-1} \in \mathbb{Z}_5^{3 \times 3}\), which should have entries that are integers mod \(5\).
  3. Compute \(\det(A^{-1})\) and show that \(\det(A^{-1}) = \det(A)^{-1}\), where the inverse is with respect to multiplication mod \(5\).
Choice Exercise 8 [6] (Endomorphism ring)

Let \( (G, +) \) be an abelian group, and denote \[ \operatorname{Endo}(G) = \{\phi\colon G \to G \mid \phi \text{ is a homomorphism}\}. \] Prove that $\operatorname{Endo}(G)$, called the endomorphism ring, is a ring with under \(\boxplus\) and \(\boxtimes\), where \(\boxplus\) is pointwise addition and \(\boxtimes\) is function composition: \[ \begin{alignat*}{2} (\phi_1 \boxplus \phi_2)(g) &= (\phi_1 + \phi_2)(g) &&= \phi_1(g) + \phi_2(g) \\ (\phi_1 \boxtimes \phi_2)(g) &= (\phi_1 \circ \phi_2)(g) &&= \phi_1(\phi_2(g)). \end{alignat*} \]
