Required Exercises

Exercise 4 [5] (Group properties)
  1. Let \((G, *)\) be a group. Use the principle of mathematical induction to prove that for all $n \geq 1$ and \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n \in G\), \[ (g_1 * g_2 * \dots * g_n)^{-1} = g_n^{-1} * \dots * g_2^{-1} * g_1^{-1} \]
  2. Let \((G, *)\) be a group. Use any proof technique at all to prove that for all \(g \in G\) and $k \geq 1$, \[ (g^k)^{-1} = (g^{-1})^k. \] (We call this element $g^{-k}$.)
  3. Give an example of a group $(G,\star)$ along with two elements $g_1, g_2 \in G$ such that $(g_1 \star g_2)^{-1} \neq g_1^{-1} \star g_2^{-1}$.
  4. Prove that function composition is associative.
  5. Prove that the set of bijections $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ (i.e. those whose graphs pass the "horizontal line test") form a group under function composition $\circ$.
  6. Prove or disprove that $\{7n \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ forms a group under addition. We call this set $7\mathbb{Z}$.
  7. Prove or disprove the set of matrices of the form \[ \left\{ \begin{pmatrix}1&x\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \middle| x \in \mathbb{R} \right\} \] forms a group under matrix multiplication.
Exercise 5 [5] (Subgroups)

Given a group \(G,\star\) and an element \(g \in G\), we say that the cyclic subgroup generated by \(g\) is \[ \langle g \rangle = \{ g^k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z} \}, \] the group of words that can be written in terms of \(g\) and \(g^{-1}\) under \(\star\), the same operation as the parent group.

We can generate other subgroups, and we use an analogous notation. Given \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n \in G), the subgroup \(\langle g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n \rangle\) consists of all of the words that consist of these generators and their inverses.

  1. Let \(G = D_4\), the dihedral group of the square.
    1. Write all of the elements in the subgroup \langle rf, r^3f \rangle.
    2. Write all of the elements in the subgroup \langle r^2, f \rangle.
  2. Recall that a cyclic group (or cyclic subgroup) is a group (respectively subgroup) that can be generated by a single element. The direct product \(C_2 \times C_3\) does not look like a cyclic group, but it is! Find a single generator \(g \in C_2 \times C_3\) such that \[ C_2 \times C_3 = \langle g \rangle \]
  3. Find the order of the largest cyclic subgroup in \(S_5\), and give an example of an element that generates it.
  4. We saw that the dihedral group of the square \(D_4\) cannot be generated by a single element (i.e. \(D_4\) is not cyclic) but it can be generated by two elements: \(D_4 = \langle r, f \rangle\). Find all pairs of elements \(g_1, g_2 \in D_4\) such that \(D_4 = \langle g_1, g_2 \rangle\).
  5. Prove or disprove the following: if \(G\) is a cyclic group, and \(H \leq G\) is a subgroup of \(G\), then \(H\) must also be a cyclic group. (Hint: check in your head that your argument works both when \(G\) is a finite group, and when \(G = \mathbb{Z}\).)

    We begin by observing that we can write every element of \(7\mathbb{Z}\) in the form \(2k\) where \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\).

    The additive identity is \( 0 = 7(0) \in 7\mathbb{Z} \).

Choice Exercise 7 [≤5] (\(\LaTeX\)) This problem will have you draw a Cayley Diagram using a \(\LaTeX\) package called tikz, which is a somewhat advanced use of \(\LaTeX\). If you feel like you could keep benefitting from learning the basics, I recommend that you keep practicing with exercise HW1.6 (or another resource), and tag it under Final Choice Exercise part 7.

Students showed interest in learning how to draw a Cayley diagram with TikZ. In this exercise, I'm going to walk you through how to draw $D_6$.

  1. Make sure that you have \usepackage{tikz} at the top. We write our TikZ code in the tikzpicture environment: 
    \begin{tikzpicture}
  [...]
\end{tikzpicture}
  2. Start by drawing a node or two. (We can do a small amount of computation by wrapping things in {...}, but it's kind of fussy.) 
    \node[draw, circle] (id) at (2,0) {};
    Here [draw, circle] tells us to shape our node like a circle and draw its boundary; (id) specifies the name of the node; we're positioning it at (2,0); and {} tells us what to write in there (nothing). (We could also compute the coordinates like ({2*cos(0)}, {2*sin(0)}).)
    Put five nodes evenly spaced around the circle of radius two named, id, r, r2, ..., r5.
  3. Now we can connect these up with edges: 
    \draw[blue, thick, ->] (id)--(r);
    This tells TikZ to draw a blue thick arrow pointing from the node named id to the node named r. Draw six blue $r$ edges. (At this point, you have drawn the Cayley diagram for the cyclic group $C_6$, which is a subgroup of $D_6$.)
  4. Now add six more nodes, which I recommend you name (f), (rf), (r2f), ... (r5f). Connect these together with six more blue $r$ edges, and pay close attention to which direction the arrows go.
  5. Make some red $f$ edges to connect the inner and outer hexagons. If you want a bidirectional arrow you can do <-> instead of ->.
Choice Exercise 8 [≤5] ⭐️ (Group or not?) The star indicates that this is based on a problem that a previous student chose as their favorite problem of the term.

For one point each (up to a maximum of five), determine which of the following are groups. Only choose examples that are not obvious to you—if you can tell within a 30 seconds whether or not an example is a group, skip it.

If an example is a group, prove it; if it is not a group, determine all of the axioms that fail. When checking the axioms, it is important to check closure of the operation, meaning that if \(a, b \in G\), then \(a \star b \in G\).

  1. The natural numbers \(\mathbb N = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) under addition \((\mathbb{N}, +)\).
  2. The integers under addition \((\mathbb{Z}, +)\).
  3. The integers under subtraction \((\mathbb{Z}, -)\).
  4. The integers under multiplication \((\mathbb{Z}, \times)\).
  5. The integers under exponentiation \((\mathbb{Z}, \wedge)\).
  6. The set \(R = \{e, r, r^2\}\) of rotations in \(D_3\).
  7. The set \(F = \{f, rf, r^2f\}\) of reflections in \(D_3\).
  8. The set of \(2\times 2\) real-valued matrices under matrix multiplication \((\mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}, \cdot)\).
  9. The set $\mathcal{R} = \{1, 5, 7, 11\}$ under multiplication $(\text{mod } 12)$: \((\mathcal R, \times_{\!12})\)
  10. The set of nonzero complex numbers under multiplication \((\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}, \times)\).
  11. The set \(A = \{-1, 0, 1\}\) under (ordinary) multiplication \((A, \times)\).
  12. The set \(\mathcal{P}_2 = \{\dots, \frac{1}{16}, \frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots\}\) under ordinary multiplication \((\mathcal{P}_2, \times)\).
  13. The set \(\mathcal{B} = \{T, F\}\) under "logical and" \((\mathcal B, \land)\).
  14. The subsets of the integers under set union \((\mathcal P(\mathbb{Z}), \cup)\).
  15. The set of surjective (1-to-1) functions \(\mathcal{F} = \{ f \mid f \colon \mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}\}\) under function composition \((\mathcal F, \circ)\).
  16. The set of differentiable real-valued functions under pointwise addition \((C^1, +)\).
  17. The set \(SL_2 = \{ A \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2} \mid A^{-1} = A^T\}\) of \(2 \times 2\) real-valued matrices whose inverse is their transpose under matrix multiplication \((\mathbb{R}^{2 \times 2}, \cdot)\).
  18. The empty set under the empty function \((\emptyset, \circledcirc)\).
  19. The set \(X = \{19\}\) under \(\circledast\) where \(19 \circledast 19 = 19\): \((X, \circledast)\).
Choice Exercise 9 [≤5] (Latin Square)

Notice that the following $5 \times 5$ multiplication table has the Latin Square (sudoku) property, in that each row and column has no duplicate values. You will determine whether or not this is a Cayley table for a group.* \[ \begin{array}{c|ccccc} * & e & a & b & c & d \\ \hline e & e & a & b & c & d \\ a & a & e & c & d & b \\ b & b & d & e & a & c \\ c & c & b & d & e & a \\ d & d & c & a & b & e \end{array} \]

  1. Does this operation have an identity element? Explain why or why not.
  2. Does this operation have inverses? Explain why or why not.
  3. Is this binary operation associative? Prove why or why not.
