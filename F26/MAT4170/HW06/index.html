Exercise 4 [10] (Classifying small groups up to isomorphism)
  1. [4] Prove that group isomorphism is an equivalence relation, by showing that it is
    1. Reflexive. (i.e., For all groups \(G\), \(G \cong G\).)
    2. Symmetric. (i.e., For all groups \(G_1, G_2\), if \(G_1 \cong G_2\) then \(G_2 \cong G_1\).)
    3. Transitive. (i.e., For all groups \(G_1, G_2, G_3\), if \(G_1 \cong G_2\) and \(G_2 \cong G_3\), then \(G_1 \cong G_3\).)
  2. [3] Prove the following facts:
    1. Every group has exactly one element of order \(1\).
    2. If \(G\) is a group of order \(p\), where \(p\) is a prime then \(G\) is a cyclic group (and thus isomorphic to the cyclic group \(C_p\)), and moreover, each non-identity element works as a generator of \(G\).
    3. If \(G\) is a group of order \(4\), then \(G\) is not necessarily a cyclic group, but it is necessarily abelian. (Hint: if \(G\) is not cyclic, then use Problem 3.1 from Exam I.)
  3. [3] Here you will classify all groups up to order 11. You need to prove that all the examples you give are not isomorphic, but you can take for granted that I have given you the correct count.
    It will be useful to remember that the direct product of abelian groups is abelian.
    1. Describe the two groups of order \(4\) up to isomorphism. (You did this in the previous part!)
    2. Describe the two groups of order \(6\) up to isomorphism. (One is not abelian.)
    3. Describe the five groups of order \(8\) up to isomorphism. (Two are not abelian, and you saw one on Exam 1.)
    4. Describe the two groups of order \(9\) up to isomorphism.
    5. Describe the two groups of order \(10\) up to isomorphism. (One is not abelian.)
    6. Without looking, guess how many groups of order \(12\) you think there are, and how many groups of order \(16\) you think there are.

  1. [2] The quiz from Thursday of week 7 asked you about conjugacy under homomorphism.
    Answer key.

    Since \(g\) and \(g'\) are conjugate, there exists some \(\ell \in L\) such that \[ \ell \star g \star \ell^{-1} = g', \] and since \(\phi\) is structure preserving, \[ \phi(\ell \star g \star \ell^{-1}) = \phi(\ell) \bullet \phi(g) \bullet \phi(\ell^{-1}). \] We showed on Tuesday that if \(h = \phi(\ell)\), then \(h^{-1} = \phi(\ell^{-1})\), and thus \(\phi(g')\) is \(\phi(g)\) conjugated by \(h = \phi(\ell)\).

In the following exercises you will be looking at conjugacy classes of the symmetry group of the hexagon.

  1. Prove that $e$ is in its own conjugacy class.
  2. Find an example of $g \in D_6$ such that $r^2 = g r^4 g^{-1}$.
  3. Find an example of $g \in D_6$ such that $f = g r^2f g^{-1}$.
  4. Prove that if $\phi\colon D_6 \to H$ is a homomorphism, and $g_1$ is conjugate to $g_2$ in $D_6$, then $\phi(g_1)$ is conjugate to $\phi(g_2)$ in $H$.
  5. Prove that if $\phi\colon D_6 \to H$ is a homomorphism, $H$ is an abelian group, and $g_1$ is conjugate to $g_2$ in $D_6$, then $\phi(g_1) = \phi(g_2)$.
  6. Let $\Phi\colon D_6 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be defined by $\Phi(r) = 0$ and $\Phi(f) = 1$, and use this to show that $r^nf$ is not conjugate to $r^m$ for any integers $n$ and $m$.
  7. Let $\psi\colon D_6 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ be defined by $\psi(r) = 1$ and $\psi(f) = 0$, and use this to prove that none of the elements in $\{r, rf, r^3, r^3f, r^5, r^5f\}$ are conjugate to any of the elements in $\{e, f, r^2, r^2f, r^4, r^4f\}$.
  8. Let $\varphi\colon D_6 \to S_3$ be the homomorphism defined by $\varphi(f) = (1\ 2)$ and $\varphi(r) = (1\ 2\ 3)$. Note that $S_3$ is not an abelian group, but use this homomorphism to show that $r^3 \in D_6$ is in its own equivalence class.
  9. Partition the dihedral group of the hexagon \[ D_6 = \langle r, f \mid r^6 = f^2 = (rf)^2 = e \rangle \] into conjugacy classes. You don't have to prove every piece of this, but you do have to make a note of any claims that you made that you didn't prove.
Choice Exercise 9 [5] (Monoids ≠ groups)

In computer programming, a common data type is a string, which is broadly used to represent text, and consists of letters, punctuation, emojis, etc. Some programming languages let you concatenate two strings together with the + operation, so for example, "hello" + "world" = "helloworld", similarly "hello" + "" = "hello". The set of strings under the concatenation operation forms what's called a "monoid," but it does not form a group.

  1. Why doesn't the set of strings under concatenation form a group?
  2. Suppose that we add a new "backspace" character that we'll denote \b, which deletes the character before it so that 
    "hello" + "\bworld" = "hellworld"
    If there is no character before it, it does nothing: 
    "\b" + "hello" = "hello"
    1. Which group axioms does this satisfy, and which does it fail to satisfy? Does the set of strings (with backspace) under concatenation form a group?
    2. Look up the definition of a monoid. Briefly explain why the set of strings (without backspace) under concatenation forms a monoid and why it fails to be a monoid after including the backspace.
Choice Exercise 10 [≤15 + 5] 🌟 (Perspectives on a cube + programming)

Suppose that you have three coins, and you can flip over any subset of them and you can permute their places. Some examples of the states of these coins are HTT, THT, THT and so on. Let's call this group \(G\). We're going to show that this is related to the (Coxeter) group \(B_3\) of symmetries of the cube \[ B_3 = \langle r_1, r_2, r_3 \mid r_1^2 = r_2^2 = r_3^2 = (r_1r_2)^4 = (r_2r_3)^3 = (r_1r_3)^2 = e\rangle, \] where \(B_3\) consists of the \(48\) symmetries of the cube (including reflections).

  1. [3] Show that \(G\) can be generated by the following three elements:
    1. \(x\), which is flipping over the left coin,
    2. \(s_1\), which is swapping the left and middle coins, and
    3. \(s_2\), which is swapping the middle and right coins.
  2. [2] Show that there is a homomorphism \(\varphi\colon B_3 \to G\) with \(\varphi(r_1) = x\), \(\varphi(r_2) = s_1\), and \(\varphi(r_3) = s_2\).
  3. [1] How many ways are there to choose a face of the cube, followed by an edge on that face, followed by a vertex on that edge? (This structure of \(v \subset e \subset f\) is called a "flag." And because the cube is "flag transitive," the number of flags is equal to the number of symmetries.)
  4. [1] Prove that the homomorphism \(\varphi\colon B_3 \to G\) is an isomorphism.
  5. Here's the generalization to the symmetries of the \(n\)-dimensional hypercube: \[ B_n = \langle r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n \mid r_i^2 = (r_1r_2)^4 = (r_jr_{j+1})^3 = (r_kr_\ell)^2 = e, \text{ for } 1 < j < n \text{ and } |k - \ell| \geq 2 \rangle. \] In this case, we're dealing with \(n\) coins, and the homomorphism \(\varphi\colon G \to B_n\) sends
    • "flip over the leftmost coin" to \(r_1\) and
    • "swap coins \(k-1\) and \(k\)" to \(r_k\).
    1. [2] Show that \(B_2\) is isomorphic to \(D_4\), the dihedral group of the square.
    2. [2] Show that \(B_n\) is isomorphic to the symmetries of the $n$-coin system.
  6. [1] Spend five minutes reading about or watching a YouTube video about "Coxeter groups," and say what you learned or are curious about.
  7. [3] Prove that there is a homomorphism $\psi\colon B_3 \to GL_3(\mathbb{R})$ (the codomain is $3 \times 3$ real-valued matrices) which sends \[ \begin{align*} \psi(r_1) &= \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} \\ \psi(r_2) &= \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \\ \psi(r_3) &= \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} \end{align*} \]
  8. [5] Here's your programming problem
    1. [3] Prove (computationally) that the homomorphism $\psi$ is 1-to-1 by generating $48$ words $r_{i_1}r_{i_2} \cdots r_{i_m}$, such that no two words correspond to the same symmetry of $B_3$.
    2. [2] In Mathematica (or another program) write a function that takes in a point $(x,y,z)$ and outputs all $48$ points in the set \[ \{A \cdot (x,y,z) \mid A \in \operatorname{Im}(\psi)\}, \] where $\psi$ is the homomorphism above. Then feed these $48$ points to ConvexHullRegion to get a polyhedron with octahedral symmetry. Put in various points, and include images of at least three interesting shapes that result from this.
