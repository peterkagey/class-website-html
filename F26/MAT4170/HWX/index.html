Extra Credit Exercise 3 [10] (Field of four elements)
This problem is a continuation of extra credit Problem 4.6 from Exam 2.

Historically, \(i = \sqrt{-1}\) was "invented" to determine roots of polynomials in \(\mathbb{R}[x]\) such as \(x^2 + 4x + 5\), which has roots at \(-2 \pm i\). We're going to do a similar construction starting with a finite field.

  1. Verify that no elements of \(\mathbb{Z}_2\) are roots of the polynomial \(x^2 + x + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}_2[x]\).
  2. Let \(\alpha \not\in \mathbb{Z}_2\) be a root of the polynomial \(x^2 + x + 1\), and explain why \(\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1\).
  3. Is \(\alpha + 1\) a root of \(x^2 + x + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}_2[x]\)?
  4. Consider the ring \[ \mathbb{Z}_2[\alpha] = \{x + y\alpha \mid x, y \in \mathbb{Z}_2\}, \] show that it is a field, and make a Cayley table for \((\mathbb{Z}_2[\alpha] \setminus \{0\}, \times)\).
  5. Now let's build a bigger field! Prove that every quadratic polynomial in \(\mathbb{Z}_2[x]\) has roots in \(\mathbb{Z}_2[\alpha]\), perhaps by checking all four of them.
  6. Give a cubic polynomial in \(\mathbb{Z}_2[x]\) which does not have any roots in \(\mathbb{Z}_2[\alpha]\).
  7. Let \(\beta\) be a root of this polynomial. Determine how many elements are in \(\mathbb{Z}_2[\beta]\), and prove that \(\mathbb{Z}_2[\beta]\) forms a field and that its group of units is cyclic.
  8. Is there an element of \(\mathbb{Z}_2[\beta]\) that is a root of the polynomial \(x^2 + x + 1\)? How does \(\beta\) relate to \(\alpha\)?
Extra Credit Exercise 4 [3+7] (Rational ring extensions)
  1. [3] Explain why the ring \((\mathbb{Z}[2/3],+,\times)\) contains all elements of the form \(a/3\) for \(a \in \mathbb{Z}\), but the map \(\phi\colon(\mathbb{Z}[2/3],+) \to (\mathbb{Z},+)\) given by \(\phi(x)=3x\) is not a group isomorphism. (Hint: Look at the codomain; this is not even a properly defined map!)
  2. [7] Recall that \(\mathbb Q[\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}]\) is the smallest ring containing the rational numbers along with \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt 3\). Prove that \(\mathbb Q[\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}] = \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}]\) by doing the following.
    1. [0] Show that \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \in \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}]\). (This is "obvious".)
    2. [3] Let \(\gamma = \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3\). Find a degree-\(4\) polynomial in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\) with \(\gamma\) as a root, using the fact that \begin{align*} \gamma^1 &= \sqrt 2 + \sqrt 3 \\ \gamma^2 &= 2 \sqrt{6}+5 \\ \gamma^3 &= 11 \sqrt{2}+9 \sqrt{3} \\ \gamma^4 &= 20 \sqrt{6}+49. \end{align*} (Note: this is called the minimal polynomial of \(\gamma\), and \(\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) is called a degree-\(4\) extension of \(\mathbb{Q}\).)
    3. [1] Explain why every element of \(\mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) can be written in the form \[ a_0 + a_1\gamma + a_2\gamma^2 + a_3\gamma^3. \]
    4. [2] Show that \(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{3} \in \mathbb{Q}[\gamma]\) by finding rational coefficients such that \begin{align*} \sqrt{2} &= a_0 + a_1\gamma + a_2\gamma^2 + a_3\gamma^3 \\ \sqrt{3} &= a'_0 + a'_1\gamma + a'_2\gamma^2 + a'_3\gamma^3. \end{align*}
    5. [1] Why does this prove that \(\mathbb Q[\sqrt{2},\sqrt{3}] = \mathbb Q[\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}]\)?

Extra Credit Exercise 6 [≤10] (Groups with same orders)

Recall the idea we have often used when differentiating groups up to isomorphism. If we list out all of the orders of the elements, then the groups fail to be isomorphic if the orders fail to match. For example, the dihedral group of the square \(D_4\) has \(1\) element of order \(1\), \(5\) elements of order \(2\), and \(2\) elements of order \(4\), whereas the quaternion group \(Q_8\) has \(1\) element of order \(1\), \(1\) element of order \(2\), and \(6\) elements of order \(4\)—thus these two groups cannot be isomorphic.

This idea does not always succeed in differentiating between non-isomorphic groups—in fact, it fails for some groups of order \(16\).

  1. First, sketch a proof of the fact that if \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is a homomorphism and \(g \in G\) has finite order, then the order of \(\phi(g)\) divides the order of \(g\).
  2. Next, recall that if \(\phi\colon G \to H\) is an isomorphism, \(\phi^{-1}\colon H \to G\) is also an isomorphism. Explain why this implies that for any finite-order \(h \in H\), the order of \(\phi^{-1}(h)\) divides the order of \(h\), and use this to prove that the order of \(\phi(g)\) is equal to the order of \(g\) for every finite-order \(g \in G\).
  3. Compute the orders of all sixteen elements of \(C_4 \times C_4\) and all sixteen elements of \(C_2 \times Q_8\) to show that the groups have the same number of elements of each order.
  4. Prove that the groups \(C_4 \times C_4\) and \(C_2 \times Q_8\) are not isomorphic.

Extra Credit Exercise 8 [5] (Direct product)

The direct product of groups is a way of combining two groups into a new group. The notation can look a bit intimidating, but the basic idea is simple: just treat the groups independently. Here's the formal definition, but keep in mind that \(*\), \(\star\), and \(\odot\) are just names for binary operations like \(+\), \(\times\), and \(\circ\).

Given two groups $(G_1,*)$ and $(G_2,\star)$, we define the direct product of groups as \[ G_1 \times G_2 = \{(g_1, g_2) \mid g_1 \in G_1 \text{ and } g_2 \in G_2\} \] with the binary operation, \(\odot\), where \[ (g_1, g_2)\odot(g'_1,g'_2) = (g_1 * g'_1, g_2 \star g'_2). \]

  1. Let \({(G_1, *) = (D_4, *)}\) be the dihedral group of the square, and let \({(G_2, \star) = (S_3, \circ)}\) be the symmetric group under function composition. Show that in \(G_1 \times G_2\), \[ \left(f, (132) \right) \odot \left(r, (23) \right) = \left(r^3f, (13) \right). \]
  2. Prove that for any two groups $G_1$ and $G_2$, the direct product of groups is a group.
  3. Prove that $G_1 \times G_2$ is abelian if and only if $G_1$ and $G_2$ are both abelian.
  4. Let $\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}$ denote the group of the integers mod $k$ under addition. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$. Compute the order of $G$. Prove that $G$ has an element of order $|G|$.
  5. Let $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Compute the order of $G$, and prove that $G$ has no element of order $|G|$.
Extra Credit Exercise 9 [5] (Abelian groups)

We say that a group \((G,*)\) is an abelian group if and only if \( a * b = b * a \) for all \(a, b \in G\).

  1. Prove that if \(G\) is generated by \(g_1\) and \(g_2\) then \(G\) is abelian if and only if \(g_1 * g_2 = g_2 * g_1\).
  2. Suppose \(G\) is generated by \(g_1, g_2, \dots, g_n\). Do you think that \(G\) is abelian if and only if \(g_i * g_j = g_j * g_i\) for all \(i, j \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\)? Explain your reasoning, but you do not have to provide a proof.
  3. Prove that for each \(n\) the cyclic group \(C_n\) is abelian.
  4. Prove that the general linear group of \(n \times n\) real-valued matrices \[ GL_n(\mathbb{R}) = \{A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \mid \det(A) \neq 0\} \] is not abelian for \(n \geq 2\).
  5. Prove that the dihedral group \(D_n\) is abelian if and only if \(n \leq 2\).
Extra Credit Exercise 10 [10] (Burnside's Lemma)

This problem will have you solve a natural problem in combinatorics where you will count something that I don't think anyone has ever counted before. It is closely related to a problem that I solved with a bunch of middle school students during a talk at Camp Conway last summer.

How many ways are there to fill the diagram for the $n$-th centered square number (illustrated below) with tiles that consist of either a horizontal or vertical stripe such as or .

The diagram for the \( n \)-th centered square number can be defined by placing unit squares centered at all points \( (x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 \) satisfying \( |x| + |y| < n \). Let \( a(n) \) be the number of ways of tiling the diagram for the \( n \)-th centered square number up to the symmetries of \( D_4 \), which are the rotations and reflections of the square.

  1. Prove by induction or any other method that the number of squares in the diagram is $n^2 + (n-1)^2$, and describe the diagram for the first centered square number.
  2. Compute $a(1)$ by "brute-force," drawing all of the possible ways of tiling the square up to rotation/reflection.
  3. Compute \( a(2) \) using Burnside's lemma. (Note, you might just do the next part first.)
  4. Give a formula for \( a(n) \) using Burnside's lemma, and check that it specializes to give the correct value for \( a(1). \)
  5. Compute explicit numerical values for \( a(3) \), \( a(4) \), and \( a(5) \). (Using a calculator like WolframAlpha might be helpful here!)
  6. $a(n) \neq 2^{n^2 + (n-1)^2 - 3}$, but it's an "asymptotically close" approximation. What is the relative error between $a(5)$ and the estimation? When \(n = 5\), which is bigger: \( a(n) \) or \( 2^{n^2 + (n-1)^2 - 3} \)?
  7. Look up the sequence \( a(1), a(2), a(3), a(4), a(5) \) on the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer sequences, and see if it's there!
Extra Credit Exercise 11 [10] (Group actions)

We call a group action faithful if for all nonidentity elements \( g \in G \setminus \{e\} \), there exists \( x \in X \) such that \( g \cdot x \neq x \). Informally, every non-identity element does something.

  1. [2] Let $X = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ and let $G = C_6$. Give an example of a faithful group action of $G$ on $X$. (Hint: is there a one-to-one homomorphism $\phi\colon C_6 \to S_5$?)
  2. [3] Let \( (G, *) \) be a group that acts on a set \( X \) and let \[ N = \{ g \in G \mid g \cdot x = x \text{ for all } x \in X \}. \] Prove that \( N \) is a normal subgroup.
  3. [3] We care about normal subgroups because we can quotient by them. Show that \( G/N \) acts on \( X \) (denoted \( \bullet \)) by \( gN \bullet x = g \cdot x, \) and that this map is well-defined: if \(g_1N = g_2N\) then \(g_1 \cdot x = g_2 \cdot x\).
  4. [2] Prove that the action of \( G/N \) is faithful.
